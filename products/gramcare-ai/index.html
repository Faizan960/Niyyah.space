GramCare AI

From field data to community health intelligence.

GramCare AI is an offline-first health-monitoring concept for rural and low-connectivity communities. Health workers would record symptoms without a network, get AI-assisted symptom analysis on the device, and synchronise later; a central system would look for time trends, location patterns and case clusters, and raise potential outbreak alerts for medical officers to review.

The problem

A pattern across villages is hard to see from inside one.

Who has it. Community health workers in rural and low-connectivity areas, and the medical officers and public-health teams who depend on their reports.

What goes wrong. Connectivity is intermittent; records are paper-based or fragmented; reporting is delayed; a worker may see several similar cases in one village but cannot easily compare them with neighbouring villages; manual analysis is slow.

Consequence. Unusual increases may only become visible after delays, when action is harder.

Evidence status. The project cites a literature review of eight IEEE papers and frames its gap as an integration gap (offline field collection plus symptom analysis plus community-level monitoring in one system), not a new algorithm. No field study or pilot has been run.
Decision support, not diagnosis. It is a prototype and does not replace clinical judgement or formal public-health surveillance.

The solution

Collect offline, analyse locally, review centrally.

A mobile field app stays usable with no network. It stores records locally, runs a lightweight symptom classifier on the device, and queues records for synchronisation. When connectivity returns, a backend collects records from many locations and a dashboard shows trends, maps and potential clusters. When configured conditions suggest an unusual pattern, it raises a Potential Outbreak Alert that qualified personnel investigate. The system never confirms an outbreak itself.

Business logic

The proposed workflow, from field record to human review.

This is the architecture described in the project documentation. None of it is built yet, so it is a design, not a description of running software.

  1. InputRecord in the field

    An authorised health worker records the patient and symptom data; the app validates it.

  2. ProcessSave locally

    Stored on the device with sync_status = PENDING when there is no connection.

  3. ProcessAnalyse on the device

    A lightweight model suggests a possible condition with confidence. It is not a diagnosis.

  4. DecisionConnectivity returns?

    Queued records are sent to the sync API; otherwise work continues offline.

  5. ProcessValidate and store

    The backend validates records and stores them in PostgreSQL; duplicates and retries must be handled.

  6. ProcessAggregate

    Cases are aggregated by village, week and condition.

  7. ProcessLook for signals

    Time trend, location concentration and case cluster logic runs on the aggregates.

  8. DecisionPattern worth review?

    If the dimensions intersect (same condition, nearby location, rising count, short window), raise a potential alert.

  9. OutputHuman review

    A medical officer sees the alert with reasoning and decides what to do.

Where it can fail or fall short

  • Offline for a long time. Records pile up as pending; the dashboard sees stale data, so data freshness must be visible.
  • Duplicates and conflicts. The synchronisation conflict-resolution strategy is explicitly undecided.
  • Model is wrong or uncertain. A tree-based model trained on a public dataset may not transfer to real field data; no validation has been done.
  • Thresholds untested. Baseline and threshold values are to be tuned on synthetic data and recorded.
  • False alarms or missed signals. An alert is a prompt for review, never confirmation.

Direct user: health workers. Reviewer: medical officers. Potential institutional purchaser: health organisations or departments. Beneficiary: communities. They are four different groups, and these are target-market hypotheses rather than existing clients or partnerships.

Turn scattered field records into a community-level picture that supports earlier human review, without removing the human.

Why offline-first and on-device

Field work cannot wait for a connection. Running a small tree model locally means analysis works offline, needs little compute, and can be explained.

How a model output becomes an alert

The model suggests a possible condition per patient. Aggregates by village, week and condition feed threshold logic; only when time, location and cluster signals intersect does a potential alert appear, with reasoning, for human review.

Model evaluation

No performance numbers exist, and none are claimed. The plan is a held-out evaluation reporting accuracy, precision, recall, F1 and a confusion matrix, and recording model versions. Until then the model is unvalidated on representative real-world data.

Demo with synthetic data

The planned demo uses fictional data only, for example a simulated rise in similar cases in one village to exercise the alert logic. That is not evidence of real-world outbreak prediction.
